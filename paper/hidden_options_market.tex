\title{\textbf{The Hidden Options Market}\\[0.4em]
\large How Much Option Convexity Listed Chains Miss, and Whether It Moves Prices}

\author{Anonymous}

\date{8 November 2026}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
FLEX options are exchange options with negotiated terms, cleared by the Options Clearing
Corporation and absent from the listed chains most option research is built on. We
assemble OCC's daily FLEX open-interest report into a panel of \PanelDates{} activity
dates and \PanelUnderlyings{} underlyings, \PanelStart{} to \PanelEnd{}. The market is
large, \ValueEnd{} of mark value on the last date, and in a census of
\HiddenCensusUnderlyings{} underlyings it is at least a tenth of option open interest for
\HiddenCensusTen{}. Counted gross, it is \GammaMedianGross{} of the median underlying's
option gamma, but most of that sits in same-strike pairs, forwards with no convexity:
netted, the share is \GammaMedianBoth{}, and large only in a few products such as SPY
and NDX. In tests specified in advance, netted FLEX dollar gamma does not predict
next-day volatility (minimum detectable effect \GPredMDEShare{} of mean absolute return
per standard deviation), and stocks do not pin to large FLEX strikes on dates when only
FLEX options expire. The hidden books are small next to trading. Two measurement results travel
further: off-grid strikes miss \FlexOnGridShare{} of FLEX series, and strike notional
overstates delta exposure at least \PosGrossMedian{}-fold for the median underlying.
\vspace{0.8em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} FLEX options; clearing data; open interest; hidden
inventory; dealer gamma; hedging; option market structure.

\noindent\textbf{JEL:} G13, G14, G23.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Most empirical work on option markets observes the listed chain: the set of
exchange-listed series with continuously disseminated quotes. FLEX options are not in
it. Their strike, expiry, exercise style and settlement are chosen by the counterparties;
the trades are executed on an exchange, typically as negotiated crosses, and cleared
centrally like any listed option. What they lack is a place in the standard listed
chains, and so in the data most empirical option research uses.

They are nonetheless publicly reported. The Options Clearing Corporation publishes a
daily FLEX open-interest report --- one for equity classes and one for index classes
--- giving symbol, right, expiry, strike, mark price and open interest for every
cleared FLEX series, with roughly twenty months of history. The one study we know of
that uses OCC's FLEX files, \citet{prakash2026}, examines 53 FLEX events in 41
single names outside the S\&P 500 between March 2025 and March 2026, with event-study
designs on listed open interest, implied volatility and returns. We use the whole daily
report, for every underlying, over twenty-three months, and ask what the market is and
whether its convexity matters.

This paper assembles that report into a panel and asks what is in it and whether it
matters.

\paragraph{What the market looks like.} It is large, and far larger than when the panel
begins, though it has barely grown in the latest cycles. Across \PanelDates{} activity
dates from \PanelStart{} to \PanelEnd{}, a one-break fit dates the turn to
\BreakMonth{}: averaged over a monthly expiry cycle, open interest was \OIFlatLevel{}
million contracts before it and \OICycleLatest{} million in the latest cycle,
\GrowthMultiple{} times as much, with the latest quarter adding $\OIQuarterChange$. On
the last date, \SeriesEnd{} series across \UnderlyingsEnd{}
underlyings carry \OIEnd{} million contracts worth \ValueEnd{} at the report's marks.
The ten largest underlyings hold \TopTenShare{} of that open interest, down from
\TopTenShareStart{} when the panel begins, and calls are \CallShare{} of it. Much of
the book is near expiry: on an average date \WithinSixtyMean{} of open interest expires
within sixty days. A long-dated tail has grown beside it, from \OverOneYearStart{} of
open interest more than a year from expiry on the first date to \OverOneYear{} on the
last. The market's monthly sawtooth is its expiry cycle: of the ten largest one-day
falls in open interest, \DropsOnExpiry{} land on a standard monthly expiry or the
activity date after it, where only \ExpiryWindowShare{} of all day-to-day changes do.

\paragraph{How much of each market it is.} Size alone does not say what listed-only
work misses, so we set FLEX against listed open interest one underlying at a time
(Section~\ref{sec:hidden}). In a census of \HiddenCensusUnderlyings{} underlyings with
listed weekly options the median FLEX share is \HiddenCensusMedian{}; \HiddenCensusTen{}
underlyings have at least a tenth of their open interest in FLEX and \HiddenCensusHalf{}
at least half. A share on one date could flatter, so we count again over
\HiddenDailyDates{} settlements: for \HiddenStableTen{} of the \HiddenDailyComplete{}
underlyings we follow that are observed on all of them (of \HiddenDailyUniverse{} in
all), FLEX is at least a tenth of open interest on every one of
those settlements, and for \HiddenStableHalfNames{} at least half, counting only series
that expire after each date. Most of these
underlyings were chosen for the option-income programmes written on them, and
\HiddenStableTenProgramme{} of the \HiddenCompleteProgramme{} programme underlyings clear
the 10\% mark on every date, against \HiddenStableTenControl{} of the
\HiddenCompleteControl{} controls. Valued at the report's marks and at listed quotes,
FLEX holds \HiddenValueLow{} to \HiddenValueHigh{} of the option value of the
underlyings we follow, against \HiddenValueOpenInterestLow{} to
\HiddenValueOpenInterestHigh{} of their open interest. The gap comes mostly from deep
in-the-money legs and from call and put lines paired at the same strike: on
\HiddenValueCompositionDate{}, \HiddenValueIntrinsic{} of the \HiddenValueFlexTotal{} of
FLEX value on these underlyings is intrinsic, \HiddenValueDeep{} sits in series worth more than
\HiddenValueDeepThreshold{} intrinsic and \HiddenValuePaired{} in paired lines
(Section~\ref{sec:hidden}). Much of the value, that is, is financing rather than risk.

\paragraph{How much convexity it holds.} Contracts are not risk. What a dealer on the
other side of a FLEX trade hedges is its gamma near spot, and gamma figures in use are
built from listed chains (Section~\ref{sec:gamma}). Pricing listed and FLEX series on one
listed volatility surface, FLEX is \GammaMedianGross{} of the median underlying's option
gamma counted gross, and \GammaMedianBoth{} once same-strike call and put pairs, forwards
with no gamma, are netted on both sides. The omission survives netting in a few products
where FLEX holds outright options: \GammaSPYBoth{} of SPY's gamma and \GammaNDXBoth{} of
NDX's.

\paragraph{Whether it predicts anything.} If dealers' hedging of FLEX books moved
underlying prices, gamma observed at date $T$, published overnight and so available at
$T+1$, should carry information about what follows. In a test fixed in advance, on
\GPredDays{} underlying-days, netted FLEX dollar gamma scaled by dollar volume does not
predict next-day absolute returns ($t=\GPredT$, permutation $p=\GPredPermP$), and the
minimum detectable effect is \GPredMDE{} basis points per standard deviation,
\GPredMDEShare{} of the mean (Section~\ref{sec:gamma-pred}). The reason is scale: the
hedge a 1\% move would call for is \GPredMedianX{} basis points of a day's dollar volume
in the median underlying-day. Gross open interest does not predict either, but that
test counts contracts, most of them forwards, and is the weaker of the two. Nor do
stocks pin to large FLEX strikes on the \PinEvents{} dates when only FLEX options expire
(Section~\ref{sec:pinning}). That design needs no listed data at all: the minimum
detectable pull is \PinMDE{} daily standard deviations.

\paragraph{Two measurement results.} Both emerged from building the census and both
apply beyond FLEX.

The first concerns identification. Before locating the authoritative report we
identified FLEX indirectly, reasoning that listed strikes fall on a standard grid so an
off-grid strike must be FLEX. Scored against OCC's own labels on \CensusSeries{} census
series, that heuristic has precision \RulePrecision{} and recall \RuleRecall{}: it misses
\FlexOnGridShare{} of FLEX series, because FLEX is frequently struck \emph{on} the grid,
and \RuleFalseShare{} of what it flags is not FLEX (Section~\ref{sec:identification}).
We report this because the reasoning is natural and the failure is not visible without
ground truth.

The second concerns counting. Open interest is reported per series; a position is not a
series. On an average date \PosStructuredMean{} of FLEX open interest sits in groups of series
with near-equal counts at one expiry, against \PosChance{} if the counts are dealt at random.
Separately, strike notional, the usual size of an open-interest book, overstates its delta
exposure: where a validated delta exists, the median underlying's strike notional is at
least \PosGrossMedian{} times its delta exposure, whatever the signs of the legs. Part of
that is a property of any single option, whose delta is below one, and part is netting
within structures, which accounts for \PosNettingShare{} of the logarithmic gap under the
all-long reading (Section~\ref{sec:notional}). On single positions the error is \PosButterflyTimes{}-fold on
a butterfly and \PosCollarTimes{}-fold on a collar, and on a deep in-the-money call it is
\PosDeepTimes{}-fold the other way. Any study using open interest is exposed to the same
conflation.

\paragraph{Relation to the literature.} Work inferring option-market positioning from
listed data must assume who holds each side: \citet{bollen2004} read net buying pressure
from the implied volatility function, \citet{garleanu2009} model demand pressure and
estimate it from data identifying end users and intermediaries, and \citet{ni2005} use
strike-level open interest to identify price clustering at expiration. Each takes the
observable series set as given. We add a category of series that listed data omits, and
show that a natural way to find it without the authoritative source fails. We do not
solve the sign problem: our gamma is netted for forwards but not signed.
\citet{park2025} find, from the funds' monthly N-PORT filings, that aggregate call selling
by option-income funds, one of the identifiable users of FLEX, predicts lower
subsequent volatility. Our test asks a narrower question at a finer grain, whether the
hidden book's gamma moves the next day's price, and finds no effect at that horizon.
The two results need not conflict: theirs is about supply pressure over weeks and the
option market's pricing of it, ours about daily hedging flow in the underlying.

Our gamma test belongs with work on whether option hedging moves the underlying.
\citet{ni2021} find that option trading affects underlying stock prices broadly through
hedge rebalancing, \citet{baltussen2021} trace intraday momentum to the hedging demand of
short-gamma positions, and \citet{ni2005}, \citet{avellaneda2003} and \citet{golez2012}
document and model pinning at expiration. \citet{lakonishok2007} and \citet{muravyev2016}
study who trades listed options and what their order flow does to prices. All of these
observe listed positions only. Our measurement says how much convexity that leaves out,
and our test bounds what the hidden part does at daily resolution. FLEX itself was
designed partly to move large equity exposures with less market impact
\citep{angel1997}, which is consistent with what we find.

\section{Data}

\subsection{The report}

OCC's FLEX open-interest report is fixed-layout text carrying, for each cleared FLEX
series: root, right, expiry, strike, mark price and open interest, grouped by option
class with per-class totals. FLEX roots are the listed root with a numeric prefix
(\texttt{1AAL}, \texttt{2DJX}, \texttt{4SPY}), which identifies them exactly.

We parse both the equity and index reports and validate each against the totals the
report itself carries. On the reference date every class total reconciles.

Files are stored under the report's own activity date rather than the date they were
fetched. This is not pedantry: OCC publishes overnight, so a capture taken during a
session returns the previous settlement, and two of our early captures were
byte-identical across every series for exactly this reason. Partitioning by capture
date would have entered one settlement into the panel twice as two apparently stable
days.

Exchange rules allow FLEX open interest in a series whose terms match a listed series to
be consolidated into the listed one. If that were common, some FLEX open interest would
leave the report before expiry by becoming listed, and the boundary of what is hidden
would move. We check on the \ConvSettlements{} settlements with stored chains, which give
listed open interest per series. \ConvTwinShare{} of FLEX open interest sits in series
with a listed twin, the same underlying, expiry, right and strike. Of the
\ConvDropped{} million FLEX contracts that leave the report before expiry, \ConvMatched{}
leave on a settlement when the listed twin gains at least \ConvMatchShare{} as many. The
same matching applied to FLEX open interest that is \emph{created}, which no
consolidation explains, gives \ConvCreatedMatched{}. Consolidation, then, is rare enough
to be indistinguishable from coincidence here, and we treat the report's FLEX series as
the hidden book.

\subsection{Size and growth}
\label{sec:growth}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/growth.pdf}
\caption{FLEX open interest (top) and its mark value (bottom) on every activity date
from \PanelStart{} to \PanelEnd{}. Faint vertical lines mark the standard monthly
expiries. The coloured line is the break from a one-break fit to log open interest.}
\label{fig:growth}
\end{figure}

Read off the two ends of the panel (Table~\ref{tab:panel}), open interest grew from
\OIStart{} to \OIEnd{} million contracts and mark value from \ValueStart{} to
\ValueEnd{}. Two single days are a poor guide to growth in this
market, though, because open interest has a monthly sawtooth (Figure~\ref{fig:growth})
and the two ends fall at different points of it. We measure growth in two ways that
do not depend on the day chosen.

The first is a one-break fit. We regress log open interest on calendar time with a
single change of slope, continuous at the break, and choose the break by least squares
over every activity date with at least forty dates on either side. The break falls on
\BreakDate{}. The fitted trend runs at $\PreBreakGrowth$ a year before it and
$\PostBreakGrowth$ a year after. The date is not an artefact of the sawtooth: fitted
to the first activity date after each monthly expiry alone, or to the last date before
each expiry alone, the same model moves the break by at most \BreakShiftDays{} days. We
date the turn on open interest rather than mark value, which also moves with the
prices of the underlyings.

The second is the average over each complete expiry cycle, from the day after one
monthly expiry to the next. Before the break the cycle averages lie between
\OIFlatLow{} and \OIFlatHigh{} million contracts, a mean of \OIFlatLevel{} million, and
they stay inside that band through the cycle ending \FlatBandEnd{}: the fitted break
dates a change of slope, not the first cycle above the old level. The latest complete
cycle, which ended on \OICycleLatestEnd{}, averages \OICycleLatest{}
million, \GrowthMultiple{} times that level. The highest reading on any single date is
\OIPeak{} million, on \OIPeakDate{}.

The fit is descriptive. It dates the turn and says nothing about its cause, and one
break cannot capture the most recent cycles, which have barely grown: the latest cycle
average is $\OIQuarterChange$ on the one a quarter earlier, where the trend fitted after
the break would give $\PostBreakGrowthQuarter$.

\subsection{The expiry cycle}
\label{sec:lifecycle}

The sawtooth in Figure~\ref{fig:growth} is the expiry cycle. Open interest builds
through each month and falls when the standard monthly expiry, the third Friday,
clears. The fall is spread over two reports: an expiring series is still listed on its
expiry date with part of its open interest, and gone on the next activity date. Of the
ten largest one-day falls in the panel, measured against the previous date's level,
\DropsOnExpiry{} land on a monthly expiry or the activity date after it:
\DropsOnExpiryDate{} on the expiry date and \DropsDayAfter{} on the date after. Few would
by chance, since only \ExpiryWindowChanges{} of the \DailyChanges{} day-to-day changes in
the panel, \ExpiryWindowShare{}, span an expiry in this way. The ten falls come from
\DropExpiries{} expiries, and each removes between \DropLow{} and \DropHigh{} of open
interest. On each of those days the series expiring lose more open interest than the market as a whole, between
\ExpiringRatioLow{} and \ExpiringRatioHigh{} times the net fall; the rest of the book
grew on the same days.

Behind that front sits a long-dated tail that has grown faster than the market: open interest more than a year from expiry was

\subsection{How much of each market is hidden}
\label{sec:hidden}

A count of contracts says little about what listed-only research misses, because the
listed market is large too. The comparison has to be made one underlying at a time.
OCC's series search returns every series it clears on an underlying, listed and FLEX
together, the FLEX products under the listed root with a leading digit. An underlying's
FLEX share is its FLEX open interest over all of its open interest.

We measure it twice. The census is one search across the \HiddenCensusSymbols{} symbols
with listed weekly options. The daily set is the \HiddenDailyUniverse{} underlyings our
collector follows: \HiddenDailyProgramme{} chosen for the option-income programmes
written on them and \HiddenDailyControl{} liquid names chosen as controls.

A search shows the latest settlement OCC has processed, which during or soon after a
session is the previous one, so the date a capture was taken does not say which
settlement it holds. We date each capture by its listed side, which the FLEX report does
not touch. A Cboe chain carries a quote timestamp and the open interest of the
settlement before that day, and for every capture kept at least \HiddenDailySupportLow{}
of the \HiddenDailyCompared{} symbols the two sources share match one chain's listed open
interest series for series. The census holds the settlement of \HiddenCensusDate{}.

The daily captures come from two places: \HiddenDailyLocalCaptures{} from our own
collector, which holds \HiddenDailyLocalSettlements{} settlements, and
\HiddenDailyCICaptures{} from a second, scheduled run of the same collector, which adds
\HiddenDailyFromCI{} more. Together they hold \HiddenDailyDates{} of the
\HiddenDailySpan{} settlements from \HiddenDailyFirst{} to \HiddenDailyLast{}; missing
are \HiddenDailyMissingDates{}. On the \HiddenDailyBothRoots{} settlements both hold,
all \HiddenDailyCrossRoot{} readings of a symbol from the second run equal our own, and
a capture that repeated a settlement with different open interest would stop the
analysis. Set aside, because they cannot be dated, are captures taken while OCC was
publishing, whose symbols hold two different settlements (\HiddenDailyMixed{}), and
captures whose settlement no chain reading covers (\HiddenDailyUndated{}). With the dates fixed
this way, the FLEX side of each capture is a second reading of the FLEX report: on
\HiddenReconAgreeing{} of the \HiddenReconSymbolDates{} symbol-dates where either
source shows FLEX, the two agree to the contract, and the largest relative difference
is \HiddenReconMax{}. A median difference above 1\% would stop the analysis.

Two kinds of product need a rule. An adjusted-deliverable class, renamed with a trailing
digit after a corporate action changed what a contract delivers (\texttt{HON2},
\texttt{1HON2}), is an underlying of its own; we neither fold it into its parent nor
count it below. The census has \HiddenAdjustedCensus{} such classes, \HiddenAdjustedCensusFlex{}
of them with FLEX open interest (\HiddenAdjustedCensusNames{}), together
\HiddenAdjustedCensusShare{} of the census's FLEX open interest. Neither the census nor
the daily captures include SPX, NDX or RUT, so for those three the listed side comes
from the Cboe chain instead, counting the monthly product and its
weekly sibling (SPX and SPXW, NDX and NDXP, RUT and RUTW) against every FLEX root on the
index; mini and micro products such as XSP stay separate. A chain read after a
settlement no longer lists the series that expired on it, so for the indices both sides
keep only series expiring after the date. Where the full book is known, that
restriction moves an equity share by at most \HiddenExpiryMax{} percentage points, and
by more than one point on \HiddenExpiryMoved{} of \HiddenExpiryNameDates{}
underlying-dates.

One date could flatter, and the daily set shows how far a share moves. Of the
\HiddenDailyComplete{} underlyings observed on all \HiddenDailyDates{} settlements (left

\HiddenStableTen{} have a FLEX share of at least 10\% on every one of them, and

\HiddenStableTenProgramme{} of the \HiddenCompleteProgramme{} programme underlyings

In value the hidden share is larger. We value listed series at their mid quote, or at
Cboe's theoretical value where the quote is one-sided or crossed (\HiddenValueTheo{} of
listed open interest), and FLEX series at the report's mark. On the
\HiddenValueCompleteDates{} dates when all \HiddenValueNames{} followed underlyings are
priced, FLEX carries between \HiddenValueLow{} and \HiddenValueHigh{} of their option
value, against \HiddenValueOpenInterestLow{} to \HiddenValueOpenInterestHigh{} of their
open interest. The median underlying's value share is \HiddenValueMedianName{}, against
\HiddenValueMedianNameOpenInterest{} of open interest, and value is the larger share for
\HiddenValueAbove{} of the \HiddenValueNames{}. Value weighs deep in-the-money legs
heavily (Section~\ref{sec:positions}), and the FLEX book holds many of them. On
\HiddenValueCompositionDate{}, \HiddenValueIntrinsic{} of the \HiddenValueFlexTotal{} of
FLEX value on these underlyings is intrinsic at that day's spot. Of the FLEX value,
\HiddenValueDeep{} sits in series worth more than \HiddenValueDeepThreshold{} intrinsic,
and \HiddenValuePaired{} in series whose strike and expiry carry both a call and a put
line, as conversions and boxes do. That day the largest single FLEX
series by value is the \HiddenLineSymbol{} \HiddenLineRight{} struck at
\HiddenLineStrike{} and expiring \HiddenLineExpiry{}, with \HiddenLineOpenInterest{}
contracts open beside a \HiddenLineOpposite{} line of \HiddenLineOppositeOpenInterest{}
at the same strike. It is marked at \HiddenLineValue{}, against \HiddenLineListed{} for
every listed \HiddenLineSymbol{} option together. We therefore report the value share
beside the open-interest share and not in its place. Neither is a measure of risk.
Intrinsic value and paired lines are mostly financing: a deep in-the-money pair at one
strike is a forward, whose value is large and whose convexity is none. The risk measure
is gamma. Section~\ref{sec:gamma} prices it on the listed surface and nets same-strike
pairs: FLEX is \GammaMedianGross{} of the median underlying's gamma counted gross, and
\GammaMedianBoth{} netted.

\section{Strike-based identification fails}
\label{sec:identification}

Listed strikes sit on a grid of whole dollars, halves and, on some products, quarters,
while a FLEX strike is whatever the two counterparties agree. An off-grid strike
therefore looks like a FLEX tell, and before we had OCC's FLEX report we identified FLEX
that way. The series search lets us score the rule exactly, because it labels every
series it lists: a FLEX product carries the listed root behind a leading digit.

We score it on the census of \HiddenCensusDate{} (Section~\ref{sec:hidden}). A series
here is one line of the search, a product, expiry and strike with its call and put open

leave out the \CensusAdjustedSeries{} in adjusted-deliverable classes, as before, and the
\CensusEmptySeries{} with no open interest on either side, which hold no position to
find. That leaves \CensusSeries{} series, \CensusFlexSeries{} of them FLEX.

\section{Cleared series are not economic positions}
\label{sec:positions}

Open interest is reported per series, and positions span series. That open interest mixes
the legs of combinations is not a new observation. What FLEX adds is a way to measure it
across a whole market: its contracts are custom, so the legs of one trade tend to share
a count no other series has, and some of the funds that hold them publish their holdings.
We show what the conflation costs on three positions, measure how much of the book is
built this way, check that measurement against disclosed holdings, and then value the
book three ways.

\subsection{Three positions}
\label{sec:cases}

These three were found while building an earlier census. Each is located here by its
signature and recomputed from the report. The report gives no signs, so the side of each
leg below is our reading of the construction.

\paragraph{A butterfly.} On \PosButterflyDate{}, \PosButterflyName{} carries
\PosButterflyLines{} call lines across \PosButterflyExpiries{} expiries, in proportions of
one, two and one at evenly spaced strikes, with wings of \PosButterflyWing{} contracts. Read
as separate positions they are \PosButterflyNotional{} of strike notional. Long the wings
and short the body, each butterfly can pay at most its strike spacing times its wing size,
\PosButterflyMax{} in all, so strike notional is \PosButterflyTimes{} times the most the
position can be worth.

\paragraph{A collar.} On \PosCollarDate{}, \PosCollarName{} at its \PosCollarExpiry{} expiry
carries four legs of about \PosCollarSize{} contracts: a call struck at \PosDeepStrike{},
a call and a put near the \PosCollarSpot{} spot, and a put further down. Read as a buffer
(long the deep call and the higher put, short the other two), the most it can be worth at
expiry is \PosCollarMax{}, close to contracts times spot at \PosCollarExposure{}. Its legs
add up to \PosCollarNotional{} of strike notional, \PosCollarTimes{} times as much.

\paragraph{A deep call.} The collar's first leg runs the other way. Strike times contracts
records it at \PosDeepNotional{}. OCC marks it at \PosDeepMark{}, below the floor of our
model (spot less the discounted strike, with no dividends), so it has no volatility and no
delta from us. A call struck at \PosDeepMoneyness{} of spot moves one for one with the
underlying all the same, and on that footing its exposure is \PosDeepExposure{},
\PosDeepTimes{} times its strike notional.

\subsection{Structures across the market}
\label{sec:structures}

Legs transacted together carry near-equal counts at a shared expiry. Within each underlying
and expiry we therefore sort the series by open interest, leave out those under
\PosMinContracts{} contracts, and start a new group wherever the count falls more than
\PosTolerance{} below the one before. A group of two or more series is a structure. The rule
sees equal counts and nothing else, so it cannot see a ratio: in the butterfly above it pairs
the wings at \PosButterflyGrouped{} of the \PosButterflyExpiries{} expiries and leaves the
body alone.

Run on each of the \PosDates{} report dates, it places \PosStructuredMean{} of open interest
in a structure on an average date (from \PosStructuredMin{} to \PosStructuredMax{}, and
\PosStructuredLast{} on the last). Few series carry that: on the last date \PosGroupedLast{} of
the \PosSeriesLast{} series with open interest are grouped, into \PosStructuresLast{}
structures. Nor is it spread evenly. The median underlying has \PosMedianStructured{} of its
open interest in a structure, and \PosHalfStructured{} of underlyings have at least half.
Of the open interest in structures, a call and a put at one strike hold \PosShareSynthetic{}
on an average date, two calls with two puts \PosShareFour{}, and two legs of one right
\PosShareVertical{}.

The share barely depends on the tolerance: on the last date it is \PosToleranceExact{} when
counts must match exactly and \PosToleranceWide{} at a tolerance of 10\%. Some of it would
arise by chance. Dealing each underlying's counts at random among its series, on every fifth
report date (\PosChanceDates{} dates), still gives \PosChance{}. That deal leaves the equal
counts in the pack, so it is generous to chance, but it shows the share is not all signal.

\paragraph{Against disclosed holdings.} Fund holdings can test the rule, in a narrow way.
Clearing is aggregate, so a match cannot show that a structure is a particular holder's
position. It can only ask whether series a fund holds together are grouped together, and a
match means no more than \emph{consistent with a disclosed holder}. We fixed the grouping
settings and the score before the first scored run.

The test uses the issuers' daily files for option-income funds, \PosFirstAsOf{} to
\PosLastAsOf{}: \PosFiles{} files from \PosHoldingFunds{} holding FLEX, each keyed on its own
as-of date. We drop \PosStaleFiles{} more than \PosStaleBdays{} business days behind their
capture. A file dated $D$ shows the settlement before $D$: open interest covers the fund's
contracts on \PosFloorPrevious{} of \PosFloorLegs{} held legs on the report before that date,
and on \PosFloorSame{} on the report of the date itself, so we score against the report
before. Global X's covered-call funds cannot help. Their \PosGlobalxLegs{} option lines, from
\PosGlobalxFunds{}, include \PosGlobalxOnReport{} on the FLEX report and \PosGlobalxMulti{}
holdings of two legs at one expiry.

A position that lasts is not fresh evidence each day, so each distinct structure counts once,
averaged over its dates. That leaves \PosTruth{} structures, held by \PosFunds{} on
\PosUnderlyings{} over \PosStructureDates{} structure-dates, and \PosSyntheticTruth{} of them
are a call and a put at one strike. The detector puts all of a structure's legs together
\PosGrouped{} of the time, and into a structure of the same type \PosRecall{} of the time, our
recall (Table~\ref{tab:positions}; 95\% Wilson interval \PosRecallLow{}--\PosRecallHigh{}). Of the \PosTouching{} distinct detected structures with a
leg some fund holds, \PosPrecision{} have two or more legs held by one fund. That is our
precision, and a lenient one: the mean over structures of the share of each one's dates on
which any two of its legs share a holder (\PosPrecisionLow{}--\PosPrecisionHigh{}). Under the
strict reading, every leg held by one fund, it is \PosPrecisionExact{}
(\PosPrecisionExactLow{}--\PosPrecisionExactHigh{}). The intervals count each structure as
one trial and ignore that structures cluster within funds, so they are too narrow if
anything. Scored one date at a time, the medians
over \PosScoreDates{} dates are \PosMedianRecall{} and \PosMedianPrecision{}, with recall
between \PosLowRecall{} and \PosHighRecall{}. Against the report of the file's own date the
two scores are \PosSameDayRecall{} and \PosSameDayPrecision{}. All \PosSyntheticTouching{}
detected same-strike pairs that touch a holding are consistent with a disclosed holder, and the misses on precision are
mostly groups of three legs. Recall and the lenient precision clear the bar of one half we
set in advance for making a market-wide statement at all, intervals included; the strict
precision's interval contains it. The validation rests on \PosTruth{} structures from one
fund family, almost all same-strike pairs, and does not speak for spreads or collars.

The score should not be read for more than it tests. It rests on one family of funds and
almost entirely on one kind of structure, and it is the easy case: in the median held leg the
fund has \PosHolderShare{} of the open interest, so few other holders blur the counts. What

\subsection{Pricing the hidden book}
\label{sec:pricing}

A mark says what a series is worth, not how it moves with the underlying. Read against
the underlying's price, though, it gives back the volatility that prices it, and that
volatility gives the series' delta, gamma and vega. We invert every mark on the
\IvDates{} report dates from \IvFirstDate{} to \IvLastDate{}, the dates for which we
also hold the listed chains, so that the volatilities can be checked against the listed
market.

The model makes one choice per input. European series are priced by Black and Scholes,
American ones on a \IvTreeSteps{}-step Cox--Ross--Rubinstein tree. The rate is a constant
\IvRate{} and the dividend yield is zero, so an American call is never exercised early
and is priced as its European twin. Time runs in calendar days over 365, and the search
runs over volatilities from \IvSigmaLow{} to \IvSigmaHigh{}. The exercise style is
OCC's. Index series are European, and an equity class names its style in its heading,
except that \IvUnreadShare{} of equity series-days in the panel sit under a heading that
does not. For those the product symbol says it. Wherever the heading names a style, the
symbol's leading digit agrees with it, 1 with American and 2 and 4 with European:
\IvPrefixContradict{} of the \IvPrefixMarked{} equity series-days with a named style
contradict that pairing, and \IvPrefixOther{} carry another digit. We therefore take an
unnamed style from the digit. Where the digit gives no answer too we price the series as
American, a fallback that covers \IvFallbackRows{}. An equity's spot is its close. An
index's is its level in the Cboe chain read after the close, and the mini products on
SPX, NDX and RUT take their fixed fraction of it. The Dow, MSCI and bitcoin indices, and
equity classes our daily bars do not cover, have no spot.

Of the \IvRows{} series-days, \IvOkShare{} give a volatility. Every other one keeps its
reason: a mark below the model's floor (\IvBelowIntrinsic{}), no spot (\IvNoSpot{}),
expiry on the report date (\IvExpired{}), a mark above the price at \IvSigmaHigh{}
(\IvNoConvergence{}), a zero mark (\IvZeroPrice{}). The \IvBelowIntrinsic{} marks below
the floor are mostly deep in-the-money calls on dividend payers: \IvBelowDeepCalls{} are on
calls struck below \IvDeepMoneyness{} of spot, and \IvBelowTopShare{} are on
\IvBelowTopNames{}. For such a call the floor is the spot less the discounted strike,
which without dividends is too high for a stock or index that pays them; the shortfall
is consistent with OCC marking these calls with dividends allowed for. Of the marks that
do invert, \IvAtFloor{} sit at the floor itself and take the bottom of the bracket; such
a volatility says nothing about the market, and these rows are flagged.

Where a FLEX series has a listed twin, the same underlying, expiry, right and strike in
the listed product of the same settlement, we compare its volatility with the one Cboe
quotes for the twin in each symbol's first chain reading after the close. Cboe computes
its volatilities against the stock price at the time of reading, which moves after
hours, so we set aside the \IvSpotSetAside{} of \IvSpotReadings{} equity readings whose
stock price is more than \IvSpotAgreement{} from the close. That leaves \IvMatched{}
series-days on \IvMatchedSeries{} series with a twin, of which \IvCompared{} invert.
Only \IvIndexMatched{} of them are index series-days, so the comparison speaks for
equity options.

\subsection{Notional across the market}
\label{sec:notional}

We value each underlying's book on each date three ways: strike times contracts, OCC's mark
times contracts, and delta exposure. Open interest has no sign, so delta exposure cannot be
netted with any confidence. We lead with the sum of the legs' absolute dollar deltas, which
nets nothing. No assignment of signs can give more exposure than that, so strike notional over
it is a floor on the true ratio. Beside it we give the reading if every leg were long: the
absolute sum of the dollar deltas within each detected structure, plus the absolute dollar
delta of each ungrouped leg. That rule is neither a floor nor a ceiling in general. We use a
delta only where the volatility inverts inside a validated cell, at a rate of \PosRate{}, and
leave out whole any structure with a leg that lacks one, so that each ratio sets strike
notional against exposure on the same series. The cells were validated on the \IvDates{}
dates with listed chains; here they are carried back over the whole panel. That keeps
\PosCovered{} of open interest over the panel and leaves \PosOutside{} out. Of all open
interest, \PosPriced{} has a usable delta before structures are completed, and \PosNoSpot{}
has no spot, chiefly index series before the chains begin.

\section{How much gamma do listed chains leave out?}
\label{sec:gamma}

Contracts are not risk, and neither is notional. What a dealer who has taken the other
side of an option hedges is its gamma near spot, and the gamma figures in use are built
from listed chains: practitioner gamma exposure, and in research the listed gamma
proxy of \citet{prakash2026}, the one study we know of on the same FLEX data. FLEX
series are in none of them. We ask how much option gamma such a figure leaves out, on
the \GammaSettlements{} settlements from \GammaStart{} to \GammaEnd{} for which we hold
listed chains, across their \GammaUnderlyings{} underlyings. The definitions were fixed
in a dated specification before any figure was computed; one addition made after a trial
run, the netting of listed pairs, is recorded there and reported beside the original
rules.

Both sides are priced on one footing. A listed series takes Black--Scholes gamma at its
own quoted volatility; a FLEX series reads the listed surface of the same underlying and
session at its strike and expiry, linear in log-moneyness within an expiry and in total
variance across expiries. \GammaInteriorShare{} of FLEX open interest is read inside the
surface, and \GammaPricedShare{} of live FLEX open interest is priced at all. Dollar
gamma is the change in dollar delta for a 1\% move. Open interest has no sign, so we
report gamma notional under two attribution rules rather than a position: \emph{gross}
counts every contract, and \emph{pair-netted} matches call against put open interest at
each strike and counts only the excess, because a long call and a short put at one strike
is a forward with no gamma. Section~\ref{sec:structures} showed that such pairs are most
of the FLEX book; where funds disclose their sides they are synthetic forwards. A
straddle would be understated by the rule, so it is a floor for books of that kind, not a
bound in general.

\input{tables/hidden_gamma}

Counted gross, FLEX is a large part of each market's gamma: \GammaMedianGross{} of the
total for the median underlying, with an interquartile range of \GammaLowGross{} to
\GammaHighGross{}, and at least a tenth for \GammaAtLeastTenGross{} of the
\GammaUnderlyings{} underlyings (Table~\ref{tab:gamma}). It is not deep-in-the-money
inventory: \GammaNearMoneyGross{} of FLEX dollar gamma sits within 5\% of spot, and
\GammaDaysWeekGross{} expires within a week. Most of it, however, is in same-strike pairs.
Netting them leaves \GammaMedianNetted{} for the median underlying, or \GammaMedianBoth{}
when listed pairs are netted by the same rule, and at least a tenth only for
\GammaTenBothNames{}. Pooled across underlyings and settlements, so that large books
weigh, the shares are \GammaPooledGross{}, \GammaPooledNetted{} and \GammaPooledBoth{}.
The index products split: in SPY and NDX the share barely moves with the rule
(\GammaSPYGross{} and \GammaNDXGross{} gross, \GammaSPYBoth{} and \GammaNDXBoth{} netted
on both sides), so their FLEX gamma is outright options, while in SPX it falls from
\GammaSPXGross{} to \GammaSPXBoth{}.

Under the sign convention vendors apply to listed chains, calls positive and puts
negative, adding FLEX would reverse the sign of the listed figure on \GammaFlipShare{} of
\GammaSignedDays{} underlying-days, and the FLEX term is a median \GammaSignedRelative{}
of the listed one in size. That convention is an assumption about who is long, not a
finding, and we report it only because it is what such figures use.

The figures do not rest on the surface. On the \GammaMarkShare{} of FLEX open interest
whose own mark inverts to a validated volatility (Section~\ref{sec:pricing}), gamma at
that volatility is \GammaMarkRatio{} times gamma from the listed surface, and the two
volatilities differ by a median of \GammaMarkVolGap{} points.

A listed-only gamma figure therefore misses nearly a fifth of gross gamma notional in the median
market we follow, but most of what it misses is the convexity of forwards, which is none.
Where FLEX holds outright options, as in SPY, NDX and the single names written by
option-income funds, the omission survives netting. Whether FLEX positions move the
underlying is a question about those books, signed; the next section tests the cruder
quantity, gross inventory.

\section{Does hidden inventory predict the visible market?}
\label{sec:predict}

If dealers' listed hedging of FLEX inventory matters, inventory at $T$ should say
something about the underlying once the report can be read. We test that twice: first on
contracts, the quantity the report gives, and then on the quantity the hedging channel
names, netted dollar gamma (Section~\ref{sec:gamma-pred}). The first test is the
crude one and we report it as such. We estimate
\begin{equation}
y_{i,t+1}
= \alpha_i + \delta_t
+ \beta\,\text{flex}_{i,t}
+ \gamma' \text{controls}_{i,t}
+ \varepsilon_{i,t},
\label{eq:pred}
\end{equation}
with the controls and errors of Table~\ref{tab:pred} \citep{cameron2011}. Each report meets
the returns realised from the date it could first be acted on (Section~\ref{sec:panel}),
and a change spanning a date the underlying did not report is dropped. The innovation is
log open interest less its mean over the previous twenty reports. Bars are fetched by the
equity report's tickers, and a bare index symbol names another security or none, so the
\PredIndexUnderlyings{} index underlyings are out. Of the \PredQualifying{} equity names
with at least \PredQualifyingDates{} activity dates, \PredNoBars{} have symbols the
provider does not serve. Adjusted-deliverable roots, option classes renamed with a digit
after a corporate action changed their deliverable (\texttt{GME1}), account for
\PredNoBarsAdjusted{}; share-class spellings such as \texttt{BRKB} for
\PredNoBarsShareClass{}; other tickers, delisted and renamed names among them, for
\PredNoBarsOther{}. That leaves \PredUnderlyingDays{}
underlying-days, \PredUnderlyings{} underlyings and \PredDates{} activity dates.

Nothing predicts. The innovation's coefficient on next-day absolute return is
$\PredInnovBeta\times10^{-4}$ at $t=\PredInnovT$, and $\PredOpenCloseBeta\times10^{-4}$ at

underlying-days with the largest inventory changes $t=\PredDecileT$. Later start dates
move the estimates, not the conclusion (Table~\ref{tab:windows}). The largest genuine
$|t|$ in all of these is \PredMaxAbsT{}.

The lead row is the innovation two activity dates later:
$\PredPlaceboBeta\times10^{-4}$ at $t=\PredPlaceboT$, clustered $p=\PredPlaceboClusterP$,

measures reverse flow, volatility moving later FLEX inventory, or something common to

as support for the null.

The minimum detectable effect at 80\% power is $\PredInnovMDE\times10^{-4}$ per log point
of innovation, \PredMDEShare{} of the mean next-day absolute return. The bound is on
contracts. Most FLEX contracts are forwards (Section~\ref{sec:gamma}), which carry no
convexity, and an unsigned count cannot tell dealer-long from dealer-short books, so the
null does not reach the hedging channel. The next test does.

\subsection{Gamma in place of contracts}
\label{sec:gamma-pred}

We repeat the test with the quantity the hedging channel names, under a specification
fixed in a dated log entry before any estimate: pair-netted FLEX dollar gamma, each
series priced at the volatility its own mark inverts to inside a validated cell, over the
mean dollar volume of the twenty sessions ending at $T$. Section~\ref{sec:gamma} found
gamma from the marks \GammaMarkRatio{} times gamma from the listed surface. The ratio is the dollar
delta a dealer holding the book would trade for a 1\% move, as a fraction of a day's
dollar volume. It is small: \GPredMedianX{} basis points for the median underlying-day and
\GPredNinetyX{} at the ninetieth percentile, with a standard deviation of \GPredSdX{}
basis points after winsorising. It is standardised, so coefficients are per standard
deviation, and an underlying-day with under half of its FLEX open interest priced is
left out. Two tests were fixed as of record, with Holm's adjustment across them: the
main coefficient, and its interaction with the top tercile of underlyings by gamma
intensity.

\input{tables/gamma_predictive}

Netted gamma does not predict next-day volatility (Table~\ref{tab:gamma-pred}): the
coefficient is $\GPredBeta\times10^{-4}$ per standard deviation at $t=\GPredT$,
clustered $p=\GPredP$, permutation $p=\GPredPermP$ and Holm $p=\GPredHolm$, across
\GPredDays{} underlying-days, \GPredUnderlyings{} underlyings and \GPredDates{} dates.
Nor does it in the names with the most of it ($t=\GPredTopT$). The minimum detectable
effect at 80\% power is $\GPredMDE\times10^{-4}$ per standard deviation,
\GPredMDEShare{} of the mean next-day absolute return, which under the rule fixed in
advance makes the result \GPredVerdict{}. Gross and vendor-signed gamma, the
open-to-close return and the session range agree; those rows are descriptive.

The one large coefficient is in the lead, netted gamma two dates later, at
$t=\GPredLeadT$, and it is negative. It is mechanical: a large move carries spot away
from the strikes where gamma is largest, and raises the dollar volume in the
denominator. Volatility moves measured hidden gamma, not the other way round.

At daily resolution, then, hidden gamma does not move the underlying: an effect of
$\GPredMDE\times10^{-4}$ on next-day absolute return per standard deviation of hedging
demand, \GPredSdX{} basis points of the day's dollar volume per 1\% move, would have been
found found four times in five. That leaves effects concentrated at particular strikes and
dates, which a daily aggregate could dilute. The next test looks at the one with the
sharpest prediction.

\subsection{Pinning at hidden strikes}
\label{sec:pinning}

Stocks with listed options tend to close near strikes on expiration dates
\citep{ni2005}, and the hedge rebalancing of option dealers who are net long gamma can
produce it \citep{avellaneda2003,golez2012}. If FLEX books are large and hedged, the same
should happen at FLEX strikes. Listed and FLEX strikes often coincide, and most listed
expiries are Fridays, so a test at a FLEX strike on a Friday cannot tell the two books
apart. FLEX expiries, however, can fall on any day. A Monday-to-Thursday expiry in a name
that lists no option expiring that day is one at which only FLEX options expire. We use
those, under a specification fixed in a dated log entry before any expiry-date close was
read. The entry carries one amendment, made before any outcome was seen and described
below.

For each such expiry, the pin strike is the expiring FLEX strike with the most open
interest, at least 100 contracts, within two daily standard deviations $\sigma$ of the
previous close. Closeness to it is measured against its mirror, the strike equally far
from the previous close on the other side. That comparison is unbiased under any
symmetric return, whatever the strike's distance from spot. Closes cluster at round
prices, and a pin strike is often round where its mirror is not, so the same comparison
is made on the five sessions after expiry, with the strike held fixed, and subtracted.
The sessions \emph{after} are used because sessions before expiry lie on the path to the
close at which the pin strike was chosen, which pulls them towards it by construction.
The original specification used the sessions before, and a test on simulated returns
without pinning rejected it at $p<10^{-7}$. Names with listed Monday-to-Thursday expiries
are left out (\PinExcluded{} of them, among them SPY, QQQ and the largest single names).
That leaves \PinEvents{} events on \PinDates{} expiry dates in \PinUnderlyings{}
underlyings, with a median of \PinContracts{} contracts at the pin strike.

\input{tables/pinning}

Stocks do not close nearer the largest FLEX strike when it expires
(Table~\ref{tab:pinning}). The expiry-less-after statistic is $\PinMean\sigma$ at
$t=\PinT$, with a sign-flip $p$ of \PinFlipP{} and Holm $p$ of \PinHolm{}. It is no
larger where the book is large relative to trading, top tercile less bottom
$\PinSizeDiff\sigma$ at $t=\PinSizeT$. Within a quarter of $\sigma$ of the strike the
difference is $\PinBandQuarterMean$ percentage points. The minimum detectable effect is
$\PinMDE\sigma$. In the simulations the specification reports, that is attraction
strong enough to pull about one expiry in seven halfway to the strike. The books are
small next to trading: the pin strike's open interest, in shares, is a median
\PinMedianSize{} of a day's share volume. Where the pin strike is mostly calls, as an
option-income fund's written calls would leave it, the estimate leans towards pinning
($t=\PinCallsT$), and where it is mostly puts, away ($t=\PinPutsT$). Neither is
distinguishable from zero, and both are descriptive. The same construction on Friday
FLEX expiries, when listed options expire too, gives $\PinFridayMean\sigma$
($t=\PinFridayT$).

\section{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{No dealer positions.} Open interest does not identify who is long, and
is aggregated across holders. Netting same-strike pairs removes forwards; it does not
sign what remains, so no measure here is a dealer position.
\item \textbf{A short panel.} \PanelMonths{} months, set by how far back OCC's rolling
retention reached when collection began. That is enough to date one turn in growth, and
too short to say whether the levelling of the latest cycles will last.
\item \textbf{One outcome family.} Realised volatility in the underlying, not the option
surface.
\item \textbf{Only pairs are netted in the panel.} Section~\ref{sec:positions} shows
that positions span series. The gamma test nets same-strike pairs, but spreads, collars
and other multi-strike structures enter at gross gamma.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

The FLEX market is publicly reported and larger than its absence from chain data
suggests. A one-break fit dates the start of its growth to \BreakMonth{}. Averaged over
the latest expiry cycle, its open interest is \OICycleLatest{} million contracts,
\GrowthMultiple{} times the level before, though the latest quarter added only
$\OIQuarterChange$, and on the last date it carried \ValueEnd{} of mark value. Much of
it is near expiry, with \WithinSixtyMean{} expiring within sixty days on an average
date, and its monthly sawtooth is that expiry cycle. Beside that front, the share more
than a year from expiry has grown from \OverOneYearStart{} to \OverOneYear{}.

It is also a large part of many single options markets. On every one of
\HiddenDailyDates{} settlements FLEX is at least a tenth of option open interest for
\HiddenStableTen{} of the \HiddenDailyComplete{} underlyings we follow that are observed on
every settlement, most of them chosen for the option-income programmes written on them,
and across a census of
\HiddenCensusUnderlyings{} underlyings the median share is
\HiddenCensusMedian{}.

Counted in contracts, that is a large hidden book; counted in convexity, it is smaller.
FLEX is \GammaMedianGross{} of the median underlying's gamma gross and \GammaMedianBoth{}
once forwards are netted, though it stays near a fifth of the gamma in SPY and NDX. A
gamma figure built from listed chains misses that part, and the rest of what it misses
is forwards.

Whether hidden convexity moves the visible market is a separate question, and at daily
resolution the answer is no. Netted FLEX dollar gamma, scaled by dollar volume, does not
predict next-day volatility in a test specified in advance; an effect of
\GPredMDEShare{} of the mean daily absolute return per standard deviation would have
been found four times in five. The hedging such a book calls for is a small fraction of
a day's trading. Gross inventory does not predict either. And at the one place a hidden
book could most plausibly move a price, its largest strike on a date when nothing else
expires, stocks do not pin. Intraday effects and the option surface remain open.

Two measurement findings travel further than the setting. A natural strike-based rule
for identifying FLEX misses \FlexOnGridShare{} of FLEX series, those struck on the
listing grid. Cleared series are not economic positions: \PosStructuredMean{} of open
interest sits in groups of series with near-equal counts, against \PosChance{} by chance.
And strike notional overstates delta exposure: on the \PosCovered{} of open interest with
a validated delta the median underlying's strike notional is at least \PosGrossMedian{}
times its delta exposure, whatever the signs. On single legs the error reaches
\PosDeepTimes{}-fold in the other direction.
