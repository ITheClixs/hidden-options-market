\title{\textbf{Notional Is Not Gamma}\\[0.4em]
\large Why Single-Name Option-Income Funds Imply Almost No Hedging Flow}

\author{Anonymous}

\date{18 August 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Single-name option-income exchange-traded funds have grown large relative to the stocks
they write on: at its 2025 peak one fund reported net assets of $5.7$ times its
underlying's entire daily dollar turnover. Their scale has prompted concern that
dealer hedging of these programmes moves the underlying. We show from disclosed
positions that the concern does not follow from the structure. These funds hold a
synthetic long --- a long call and a short put at effectively the same strike --- which
is a forward by put-call parity and therefore carries no gamma: the dealer hedges it
once and does not rebalance it. All rebalancing arises from the short-call overlay,
which the funds write as spreads, offsetting further. Computing dollar gamma leg by leg
across $24$ programmes from disclosed holdings, a one percent move in the underlying
implies a rehedge of between $0.00\%$ and $0.65\%$ of daily dollar volume, with a
median of $0.09\%$. Notional overstates the hedging footprint by roughly two orders of
magnitude. Consistent with this, a difference-in-differences design over $64{,}553$
name-days finds no volume response to programme size on any weekday, and a
randomisation test places the estimate at the $37$th percentile of its null; but we
show the design's minimum detectable effect is about an order of magnitude larger than
the predicted flow, so the empirical null corroborates the calculation rather than
testing it independently. Separately, we document that these positions are recoverable
end to end from free public sources --- issuer holdings reconcile exactly to Options
Clearing Corporation series open interest on $8$ of $11$ attributed legs --- and that
the FLEX leg carrying the synthetic position is identifiable from a cent-offset strike
with one-sided open interest.

\vspace{0.8em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} option-income funds; covered calls; dealer gamma; hedging
flow; FLEX options; disclosed positions.

\noindent\textbf{JEL:} G12, G14, G23.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Option-income exchange-traded funds have grown quickly, and their growth has attracted
attention from supervisors and market commentators who ask whether the hedging of these
positions amplifies movements in the underlying. The question is natural. The funds are
large: measured against the daily dollar turnover of the single stocks they write on,
the programmes in our sample reach a peak ratio of $5.7$, and seven of $23$ exceed
$0.25$. If a dealer intermediating a multi-billion-dollar position rebalances it in the
underlying, the flow should be conspicuous.

This paper makes a simple structural point and then documents its consequences. The
concern rests on an implicit step from notional to hedging flow. For these products the
step does not hold, because of how the position is built.

A single-name option-income fund does not own the stock. It holds a long call and a
short put at effectively the same strike and expiry, and writes calls against that
position. By put-call parity a long call plus a short put at one strike is a forward.
It has a delta of approximately one and, critically, \emph{a gamma of zero}. The dealer
on the other side of the synthetic acquires a static short forward, hedges it once at
inception, and thereafter has nothing to rebalance. Every unit of rebalancing flow the
programme generates must come from the short-call overlay alone --- and because these
funds write call \emph{spreads} rather than naked calls, the long leg of each spread
offsets part of the short leg's gamma as well.

The result is a large gap between notional and hedging footprint. We compute dollar
gamma leg by leg from disclosed holdings for $24$ programmes, using exchange greeks
where the series is listed and Black-Scholes where the leg is FLEX. A one percent move
in the underlying implies a dealer rehedge of between $0.00\%$ and $0.65\%$ of that
underlying's median daily dollar volume, with a median across programmes of $0.09\%$.
Even a five percent move --- a typical daily range for the most volatile names in the
sample --- implies a median rehedge near half a percent of daily volume.

We then ask whether a footprint of that size is visible in the data, and find that it
is not, using a difference-in-differences design around staggered programme launches
over $64{,}553$ name-days. The estimated response of volume to programme size is within
$\pm 0.006$ on every weekday, and a randomisation test that permutes size across
programmes places the estimate at the $37$th percentile of its own null distribution.

We are explicit about what this second result does and does not add. The design's
minimum detectable effect is about two percent of volume per log unit of programme
size, while the structural calculation predicts an effect roughly an order of magnitude
smaller. The empirical null is therefore \emph{consistent} with the calculation but does
not independently test it: a test cannot reject what it was never powered to see. We
report it because a reader is entitled to know that the data were examined and what was
found, and because the accompanying positive controls establish that the design detects
comparable effects when they exist --- volatility-driven volume at $t=9.4$ and
option-expiry effects at $|t|=3.1$ to $4.7$ in the same panel.\footnote{The empirical
design was specified and executed before the structural calculation was performed. The
project repository records that order, including a pre-registered pilot specification
and results that the calculation later reframed. We describe the work in logical rather
than chronological order here, but the chronology is available and we think it matters.}

A third contribution is methodological. We show that this category of derivative
positioning is measurable end to end from free public sources. Issuer holdings give the
intended position at contract level; Options Clearing Corporation series files give
realised open interest for every series, including FLEX series that never print on the
ordinary tape. The two reconcile exactly, to the contract, for $8$ of the $11$ legs we
attribute. The synthetic put leg, which we had expected to be unobservable without
commercial data, turns out to carry a distinctive signature: a non-standard-deliverable
series struck one or two cents off the round strike, holding one-sided open interest.

\paragraph{Relation to the literature.} \citet{cboe2024} asks whether option-income
funds suppress volatility and concludes they do not, arguing that diversity across
underlyings, tenors and strikes dilutes any surface effect; that analysis is aggregate
and, as it notes, without data on the notional or volume share these funds represent.
We supply the position-level calculation at single-name level and identify the
structural reason rather than the dilution one. \citet{park2025} find that aggregate
call overwriting by income mutual funds predicts lower implied and realised volatility
in the following month; our results concern a different outcome and do not contradict
theirs, but they do imply that any such effect operates through a channel other than
delta-hedging volume in the underlying. \citet{brogger2021} shows that the predictable
rebalancing of leveraged volatility products is absorbed in an orderly fashion; our
setting differs in that the flow we examine turns out to be small at source rather than
absorbed after the fact. On measurement, work inferring dealer positioning from open
interest under sign assumptions \citep{garleanu2009,bollen2004} must assume the side;
we read it from disclosure and verify it against clearing data.

\section{Institutional setting}

Taking the largest programme in our sample as of 17 August 2026, the disclosed
portfolio contains, alongside Treasury bills and cash:

\begin{itemize}
\item a long call struck at \$95.00 expiring 16 October 2026, $+45{,}115$ contracts;
\item a short put on a non-standard-deliverable series struck at \$95.01, same expiry,
      $-45{,}115$ contracts;
\item a ladder of long and short calls expiring 21 August 2026 spanning strikes \$94 to
      \$106, forming call spreads.
\end{itemize}

The first two legs are the synthetic long. The one-cent strike offset distinguishes the
non-standard-deliverable series from the listed one and prevents the two from being
netted at clearing. The ladder is the income overlay.

\subsection{Why the synthetic long generates no rebalancing}

By put-call parity, for a call $C$ and put $P$ at strike $K$ and maturity $T$,
\begin{equation}
C - P \;=\; S - K e^{-rT},
\end{equation}
so a long call and short put at the same strike replicate a forward on the underlying.
Differentiating twice in $S$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial^2 (C-P)}{\partial S^2} \;=\; 0 .
\end{equation}
The position has unit delta and zero gamma. A dealer short this structure holds a
static short forward: the hedge is established once and requires no rebalancing as the
underlying moves. In our sample the two legs differ by one cent in strike, which is
immaterial.

This is the crux. It means the programme's notional --- the quantity that makes these
funds look large --- is attached to the part of the position that generates no ongoing
hedging flow at all. Only the overlay matters, and the overlay is a small fraction of
notional in gamma terms.

\section{The implied hedging flow}
\label{sec:implied}

We compute, for each programme, the dollar delta and the change in dollar delta implied
by a one percent move in the underlying:
\begin{equation}
\Gamma^{\$}_{1\%} \;=\; \sum_{\ell} q_\ell \cdot 100 \cdot \gamma_\ell \cdot S \cdot (0.01\, S),
\end{equation}
summing over disclosed legs $\ell$ with signed contract counts $q_\ell$. Greeks are
taken from the exchange chain where the series is listed and computed under
Black-Scholes where the leg is FLEX. Including the FLEX legs is essential: they are
precisely the synthetic puts, and omitting them leaves the long synthetic calls
uncancelled and overstates gamma by orders of magnitude.

The dispersion is informative. Dollar delta reaches \$1.1bn for the largest programme,
and the ratio of programme net assets to daily turnover reaches $5.7$ elsewhere in the
sample. Yet the implied rebalancing never exceeds two thirds of one percent of daily
volume for a one percent move, and its median is under a tenth of a percent. Scaling to
a five percent move, which is a routine daily range for the most volatile names here,
the median implied rehedge is roughly half a percent of daily volume.

Note also the sign heterogeneity: AMDY shows positive gamma, meaning the leg
composition happens to leave the dealer short gamma rather than long. Aggregating these
programmes into a single directional ``gamma'' figure, as is common in commentary,
discards that variation.

\section{Measuring disclosed positioning from free sources}
\label{sec:measurement}

The calculation above requires knowing the positions. We describe how they are
obtained, because the procedure is reusable and costs nothing.

\paragraph{Sources.} Issuer holdings files list every position daily with a signed
quantity and an OSI-like identifier from which root, expiry, right and strike are
recovered; leading-digit roots denote non-standard deliverable series. The Options
Clearing Corporation publishes per-underlying open interest for every series including
FLEX. Full chains with two-sided quotes, sizes and greeks are available from an
exchange content delivery network, and underlying bars from a public charting
interface. Programme size over time comes from Form N-PORT.

series at one strike and expiry.

Two conditions jointly identify the leg: an offset strike, meaning the cents component
is not a standard increment, and one-sidedness, meaning essentially all open interest
in the series sits on one right. A detector keyed instead on \emph{equal} call and put
open interest recovers a different and much larger population --- conversions,
reversals and boxes, which are dealer financing structures rather than fund positions
--- and we report the two separately.

\paragraph{Reconciliation.} Across $68$ underlyings and $113{,}610$ series, matched
against $28$ programmes on underlying, expiry, strike and right, the procedure
attributes $11$ legs. Eight match the disclosed contract count exactly. Of the
remainder, one differs by $116$ contracts, consistent with a second holder in the same
series; one by $4{,}009$, consistent with a second programme at the same strike; and one
is a false positive on the call side. We report the attributed count as small: it
reflects a single observation date and a registry covering one issuer's complex, and it
should be read as a demonstration that the reconciliation works rather than as a census.

\section{Is a footprint of this size visible?}

\subsection{Data and design}

The treated group is the $25$ underlyings carrying a dedicated single-name programme,
with launch dates from each fund's first trade date, staggered between November 2022 and
July 2026. The control group is $27$ liquid names without such a programme. Daily bars
over five years give $64{,}553$ name-days across $52$ names and $1{,}253$ trading days.

Programme size is taken from N-PORT ($226$ reports across $23$ funds) and enters as a
step function that is never carried backwards before its report date. Using reported
rather than current size matters: the largest fund reported \$437m in July 2024,
\$5{,}747m in July 2025, and \$726m in August 2026.

We estimate
\begin{align}
\log V_{i,t} \;=\; \alpha_i + \delta_t
&+ \beta_1 \mathit{post}_{i,t}
 + \beta_2 (\mathit{post}_{i,t} \times \mathit{day}_t) \nonumber \\
&+ \beta_3 (\mathit{post}_{i,t} \times \mathit{day}_t \times \log \dose_{i,t})
 + \beta_4 (\mathit{post}_{i,t} \times \log \dose_{i,t}) \nonumber \\
&+ \beta_5 (\mathit{day}_t \times \mathit{treated}_i)
 + \gamma |r_{i,t}| + \varepsilon_{i,t},
\label{eq:main}
\end{align}
with $\beta_3$ the estimand. Name effects absorb persistent liquidity differences, date
effects absorb all market-wide variation including the common weekday pattern, and
$|r|$ absorbs the mechanical dependence of volume on absolute returns. The roll weekday
is scanned across all five days rather than assumed, and all five are reported.
Inference is two-way clustered on name and date \citep{cameron2011} with $t$ critical
values from the smaller clustering dimension; the estimator agrees with a reference
implementation to four decimals and is very slightly conservative in simulation under
errors with within-name persistence and a common daily factor.

The design detects comparable effects when they exist. That establishes the machinery
works; it does not establish that this test was capable of seeing the effect
Section~\ref{sec:implied} predicts. It was not. The minimum detectable effect is about
$0.020$ in log volume per log unit of programme size, whereas the predicted rehedge is
of order $0.001$ to $0.005$ of daily volume even for the largest programmes on
high-volatility days. The empirical null is therefore corroborative and not
independent evidence, and we do not present it as a rejection of anything.

The negative sign on the expiry controls reflects composition rather than programmes:
our control names are mega-caps with large index-driven expiry activity, so treated
names show less relative volume on those dates.

Pre-launch coefficients in Table~\ref{tab:event} are not flat. Volume is elevated two to
four quarters before launch, dips immediately before it, peaks two quarters after, and
decays --- the profile of an attention cycle in which sponsors launch products onto
stocks already receiving unusual interest. Consequently the level coefficients in
Table~\ref{tab:main}, including the $+2.9\%$ Friday estimate, cannot be given a causal
reading, and we do not rely on them. The size response is more robust, and the bias runs
toward detection: if larger programmes launch onto larger attention surges, $\beta_3$ is
biased upward, and we estimate zero.

\subsection{A confound that looks like a result}

Repeating the exercise intraday on $60$ sessions of five-minute bars ($40{,}560$
bin-observations), every programme launched before the window opens, so the
difference-in-differences collapses. A specification identifying from programme size
interacted with time of day, with name and date-by-bin effects, produces a clean
monotone profile: $+0.035$ at the open falling to $-0.042$ at 15:00 ($t=-3.52$) and
$-0.095$ at 15:30 ($t=-3.20$).

It is not evidence of hedging. The sign is wrong for close-concentrated hedging, and
with programme size time-invariant inside the window the interaction cannot be
separated from that of any other name characteristic with time of day. Programme size
is strongly correlated with clientele: large-programme names here are retail-heavy and
open-weighted, small-programme names are mega-caps with large closing auctions. A
within-name design, using each name's own share of daily volume in the final hour with
name and date effects, is null on all five weekdays, the largest being Wednesday at
$t=2.19$ against a Bonferroni threshold near $2.8$. We report the failed specification
because cross-sectional size sorts are common and this one is persuasive and wrong.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The gamma calculation is a snapshot.} It uses disclosed positions on one
date. Position composition varies over the roll cycle, and a fuller treatment would
integrate over it. We would expect the conclusion to be robust because it follows from
structure rather than from a particular day's strikes, but we have not shown that.

\item \textbf{Gamma is not the only hedging channel.} Vanna and charm generate flow as
volatility and time change, and we have not quantified them. Large moves also carry the
overlay through strikes, which the local calculation understates.

\item \textbf{The empirical test is underpowered for the predicted effect} and is
reported as corroborative only.

\item \textbf{Volume, not prices.} A price effect without a detectable volume effect is
difficult but not impossible, for instance through quote revision in anticipation.

\item \textbf{Roll timing is inferred} from weekday rather than observed.

\item \textbf{One product family.} The treated group is dominated by a single issuer's
complex; index-level and buffer programmes are not covered.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

Single-name option-income funds are large in notional and small in gamma, and the gap
between the two is structural rather than incidental. The synthetic long that carries
almost all of their notional is a forward, and a forward does not need rebalancing.
What remains is a short-call overlay written as spreads, implying a dealer rehedge with
a median of nine basis points of daily dollar volume per one percent move and a maximum
of sixty-five.

The practical implication is that notional, assets under management, and ratios of
either to turnover are poor guides to the market footprint of these products. The
relevant quantity is dollar gamma net of the offsetting legs, it is computable from
public disclosure, and it is roughly two orders of magnitude smaller.

We do not claim these funds are inconsequential. They may affect the price of
volatility, as \citet{park2025} suggest, through the supply of options rather than
through hedging flow in the underlying; that channel is untouched by our calculation.
What we claim is narrower and, we think, useful: the specific mechanism by which large
notional is presumed to reach the underlying does not operate at the magnitude the
notional implies, and the reason is visible in the structure of the position.
