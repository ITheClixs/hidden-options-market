\title{\textbf{A Disclosed Mechanical Option Flow Leaves No Volume Footprint}\\[0.4em]
\large Evidence from the Single-Name Option-Income Fund Complex}

\author{Anonymous}

\date{18 August 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Single-name option-income exchange-traded funds write options against a synthetic long
position on one underlying stock. Their holdings, strikes, expiries and roll calendars
are disclosed daily, their trades are mechanical, and at the extreme one fund held
$5.7$ times its underlying's entire daily dollar turnover. Standard reasoning about
dealer hedging implies these programmes should leave a large, dated footprint in the
underlying. We look for it and do not find it. Across $23$ programmes spanning three
orders of magnitude of size relative to turnover, the volume response to programme size
is statistically indistinguishable from zero on every weekday, with a minimum
detectable effect of about two percent per log unit of size; a randomisation test
places the estimate at the $37$th percentile of its own null. The same estimator on the
same panel detects volatility-driven volume at $t=9.4$ and option-expiry effects at
$|t|=3.1$ to $4.7$, so the null is a property of the flow rather than of the design.
An intraday specification that sorts names cross-sectionally by programme size produces
a monotone, highly significant profile of the wrong sign; it does not survive a
within-name design and we report it as a cautionary case. We also document that the
positions can be recovered end to end from free public sources: fund holdings reconcile
to Options Clearing Corporation series open interest exactly on $8$ of $11$ attributed
legs, and the FLEX leg that carries the synthetic position is identifiable from a
cent-offset strike with one-sided open interest. The results bound the footprint of a
large, fully disclosed, uninformed derivative flow near zero and suggest that such flow
is intermediated against offsetting demand rather than hedged into the underlying.

\vspace{0.8em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} option-income funds; covered calls; dealer hedging; market
absorption; disclosed positions; FLEX options.

\noindent\textbf{JEL:} G12, G14, G23.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A recurring claim in both practitioner and academic work is that option dealers, having
taken the other side of customer positions, hedge those positions in the underlying and
in doing so move it. The claim underlies the industry of ``dealer gamma'' estimates, a
substantial empirical literature on hedging-induced return dynamics, and recurring
supervisory attention to options-based funds as amplifiers of volatility. The claim is
difficult to test directly because dealer inventory is not observed. Studies infer it
from open interest under sign assumptions, or from exchange data that identifies trade
initiator but not the resulting book.

This paper studies a setting where the inference is unnecessary. Single-name
option-income exchange-traded funds hold a synthetic long position in one stock and
write calls against it. Three features make them unusually informative. First, their
option positions are disclosed daily at contract level: strike, expiry, right and
signed contract count. Second, the programmes are mechanical, following a schedule set
in the prospectus rather than a view on the underlying. Third, and most importantly,
several have become very large relative to the stocks they write on. At its peak in
July 2025 the largest fund in our sample reported \$5.75bn of net assets against a
median daily dollar volume in its underlying of about \$1.0bn.

If a dealer intermediating such a programme hedges it in the underlying, the resulting
flow should be conspicuous. We look for it in trading volume, which is the necessary
condition: whatever else scheduled hedging of a multi-billion-dollar delta position
does, it must transact. We find no relationship between programme size and volume. The
coefficient on the interaction of programme size with day of week is within
$\pm 0.006$ on every weekday against a minimum detectable effect near $0.020$ per log
unit, and programme size in our sample spans roughly seven log units. A randomisation
test that permutes size across programmes puts the estimate at the $37$th percentile of
its null distribution.

Two features make this a bound rather than an unrejected hypothesis. The first is
power, discussed throughout. The second is a set of positive controls: the same
estimator applied to the same panel recovers the mechanical relationship between
absolute returns and volume at $t=9.4$, a triple-witching effect at $t=-4.7$ and a
monthly option-expiry effect at $t=-3.1$. A design that detects option-expiry effects
and does not detect the programme is informative about the programme.

We make three contributions.

\paragraph{A measurement.} We show that the positions of this complex can be recovered
from free public sources and cross-validated. Issuer holdings files give the intended
position; Options Clearing Corporation series files give realised open interest for
every series including FLEX. The two reconcile exactly, to the contract, for $8$ of the
$11$ legs we attribute. The synthetic leg, which is transacted as FLEX and does not
print on the ordinary tape, is identifiable from a distinctive signature: a
non-standard-deliverable series struck one or two cents off the round strike, carrying
one-sided open interest.

\paragraph{A bound.} We bound the volume footprint of a large, disclosed, mechanical
derivative programme near zero, with power documented and positive controls passed.

\paragraph{A cautionary case.} An intraday specification that sorts names by programme
size cross-sectionally yields a monotone profile with two coefficients beyond
$|t|=3$ and the wrong sign for hedging. It is confounded: programme size is correlated
with the clientele of the underlying, and the profile reflects retail-heavy names being
open-weighted rather than anything about programmes. It disappears in a within-name
design. Since cross-sectional dose sorts are common in this literature, we report the
failure alongside its refutation.

\paragraph{Relation to the literature.} Our conclusion is close in spirit to
\citet{brogger2021} who studies leveraged volatility exchange-traded products, finds
their predictable rebalancing is absorbed in an orderly way, and reports no excess
profit from trading ahead of it. The flow we study is larger relative to its market,
disclosed in far greater detail, and concentrated in single names rather than an index
complex, and we reach the same qualitative answer through a different design.
\citet{park2025} document that aggregate call overwriting by income mutual funds
predicts lower implied and realised volatility in the following month; our results do
not contradict theirs, since we examine a different outcome at a different frequency,
but they do place a tight bound on the volume channel through which such an effect
would most naturally operate. On the measurement side our approach is complementary to
work that infers positioning from open interest under sign assumptions
\citep{garleanu2009,bollen2004}: rather than assume the sign, we read it from
disclosure and then verify it against clearing data.

\section{Institutional setting}

Single-name option-income funds do not hold the underlying stock. They construct a
synthetic long position and write options against it. Taking the largest fund in our
sample as of 17 August 2026, the disclosed portfolio contains:

\begin{itemize}
\item a long call struck at \$95 expiring 16 October 2026, $+45{,}115$ contracts;
\item a short put on a non-standard-deliverable series struck at \$95.01, same expiry,
      $-45{,}115$ contracts;
\item a ladder of short and long calls expiring 21 August 2026 spanning strikes \$94 to
      \$106, forming call spreads;
\item Treasury bills and cash comprising most of the balance sheet.
\end{itemize}

The first two legs are a synthetic long: long call plus short put at effectively the
same strike replicates the stock. The one-cent strike offset on the put distinguishes
the non-standard-deliverable series from the listed one. The call ladder is the income
overlay.

Two implications matter. First, the fund's net delta is large and positive: the
synthetic long contributes approximately one unit of delta per unit of notional, the
short calls subtract a fraction. The dealer on the other side therefore carries a
correspondingly large negative delta, which under textbook practice is hedged by buying
the underlying and rebalanced as the position's delta changes. Second, because the
overlay is short-dated and rolled frequently, the rebalancing is recurrent rather than
one-off.

The magnitude of the implied hedging can be sized. For an at-the-money option book, a
$5\%$ move in the underlying changes delta by roughly $5$ to $15$ percentage points of
notional. The largest fund's underlying has a daily volatility near $6\%$, and the fund
held between one and six times the underlying's daily dollar volume over its life.
Daily delta rebalancing of order ten percent of notional would therefore be of order
ten percent or more of the underlying's daily volume. This is the effect we look for.

\section{Measuring disclosed derivative positioning}
\label{sec:measurement}

Our measurement combines three free sources.

\paragraph{Issuer holdings.} Each fund publishes a daily holdings file listing every
position with a signed quantity. Option positions carry an identifier in an
OSI-like form from which root, expiry, right and strike are recovered. Leading-digit
roots denote non-standard deliverable series.

\paragraph{Clearing data.} The Options Clearing Corporation publishes, per underlying,
open interest for every series, including FLEX series that do not appear in ordinary
listed chains. This is the independent check on disclosure.

\paragraph{Chains and prices.} Full option chains with two-sided quotes and quote sizes
are available from an exchange content delivery network; underlying bars are available
from a public charting interface.

\subsection{Identifying the FLEX leg}

The synthetic put leg is transacted as FLEX and does not print on the ordinary tape,
which we expected to make it unobservable without commercial data. It is not. The leg
leaves a distinctive signature in clearing data. Table~\ref{tab:signature} shows the
cluster of series at one strike and expiry for the largest programme's underlying.

Two conditions jointly identify the leg: an \emph{offset strike}, meaning the cents
component is not a standard increment, and \emph{one-sidedness}, meaning essentially
all open interest in the series sits on one right. Note that a detector keyed on
\emph{equal} call and put open interest finds a different and much larger population,
consisting of conversions, reversals and boxes, which are dealer financing structures
rather than fund positions. We report both separately.

\subsection{Reconciliation}

Applying the detector to $68$ underlyings and $113{,}610$ series, and matching against
$28$ programmes' disclosed holdings on underlying, expiry, strike and right, yields
$11$ attributed legs. Eight match the disclosed contract count exactly. The three that
do not are explicable: one differs by $116$ contracts, consistent with a second holder
in the same series; one differs by $4{,}009$, consistent with a second programme on the
same underlying and strike; and one is a false positive on the call side.

We regard this as the paper's most durable component. It establishes that a
consequential category of derivative positioning is publicly measurable at no cost, and
it is independent of anything we conclude below.

\section{Data and sample}

The treated group is the $25$ underlyings carrying a dedicated single-name
option-income programme, identified from the issuer's fund registry, with launch dates
taken from each fund's first trade date. Launches are staggered from November 2022 to
July 2026. The control group is $27$ liquid names carrying no such programme.

Daily bars covering five years give $64{,}553$ name-days across $52$ names and
$1{,}253$ trading days. Programme size is taken from Form N-PORT: $226$ reports across
$23$ funds between January 2023 and April 2026. N-PORT is filed monthly and released
quarterly with roughly a two-month lag, so size enters as a step function that never
carries a value backwards before its report date.

Using reported rather than current size is essential. The largest fund reported
\$437m of net assets in July 2024, \$5{,}747m in July 2025 and \$726m in August 2026.
An earlier version of this analysis applied the current figure across the whole sample
and produced an uninformative dose sort; the correction is not cosmetic.

We define programme size relative to turnover as
\begin{equation}
\dose_{i,t} \;=\; \frac{\text{reported net assets}_{i,t}}{\text{median daily dollar volume}_i},
\end{equation}
and use $\log \dose$, demeaned, in estimation. Table~\ref{tab:dose} reports the
distribution. It spans three orders of magnitude, which is what a size-response test
requires.

\section{Empirical design}

Our outcome is $\log$ dollar volume. The specification is
\begin{align}
\log V_{i,t} \;=\; \alpha_i + \delta_t
&+ \beta_1 \, \mathit{post}_{i,t}
 + \beta_2 \, (\mathit{post}_{i,t} \times \mathit{day}_t) \nonumber \\
&+ \beta_3 \, (\mathit{post}_{i,t} \times \mathit{day}_t \times \log \dose_{i,t})
 + \beta_4 \, (\mathit{post}_{i,t} \times \log \dose_{i,t}) \nonumber \\
&+ \beta_5 \, (\mathit{day}_t \times \mathit{treated}_i)
 + \gamma \, |r_{i,t}| + \varepsilon_{i,t},
\label{eq:main}
\end{align}
where $\mathit{post}$ indicates dates after a programme's launch, $\mathit{day}$ is a
weekday indicator, and $|r|$ absorbs the mechanical dependence of volume on absolute
returns. Name effects absorb persistent liquidity differences; date effects absorb all
market-wide variation including the common weekday pattern.

The estimand is $\beta_3$: whether the day-of-week volume footprint scales with
programme size. This is the mechanism test. A flow proportional to programme notional
must generate an effect that grows with notional; an effect identical across sizes is
not that flow.

We do not assume which day the programme transacts. The roll weekday is scanned across
all five days and all five are reported. This is a five-way search and we treat it as
such.

Inference is two-way clustered on name and date following \citet{cameron2011}, with
critical values from a $t$ distribution with degrees of freedom set by the smaller
clustering dimension. We verified the estimator against a reference implementation
(agreement to four decimals on both coefficient and standard error) and checked its
calibration by simulation under errors with within-name persistence and a common daily
factor, where it is very slightly conservative.

The size response is indistinguishable from zero on every day, with every point
estimate inside $\pm 0.006$ and standard errors implying a detectable effect near
$0.020$ per log unit. Since $\log \dose$ spans approximately seven units across the
sample, the design would resolve a spread of roughly $14\%$ of volume between the
smallest and largest programmes. It finds none.

Replacing turnover measured over the full sample with turnover measured over the
pre-launch window only, which removes any endogenous contribution of the programme to
the denominator, leaves the Friday estimate at $-0.0038$ ($t=-0.41$).

\subsection{Randomisation inference}

Because the estimate relies on clustered standard errors with $25$ treated clusters, we
also conduct randomisation inference, permuting programme size across treated names and
re-estimating $300$ times. The actual estimate of $-0.00348$ sits at the $37$th
percentile of the permutation distribution, whose central $95\%$ runs from $-0.0232$ to
$+0.0201$; the two-sided permutation $p$-value is $0.773$. The permutation standard
deviation of $0.0114$ is close to the clustered standard error, so the parametric
inference was not materially optimistic.

This is important and largely negative for one of our own auxiliary results. The
pre-launch coefficients are not flat: volume in these names is elevated two to four
quarters before launch, dips in the quarter immediately preceding it, rises to a peak
two quarters after, and decays. The pattern is that of an attention cycle in which
sponsors launch products onto stocks that are already the subject of unusual interest.

The consequence is that \emph{level} effects around launch, including the $+2.9\%$
Friday coefficient in Table~\ref{tab:main}, cannot be given a causal reading. We do not
rely on them.

The size response is more robust to this concern, and the direction of the bias is
favourable to detection. If sponsors launch larger programmes onto names experiencing
larger attention surges, then programme size is positively correlated with the
confounding surge, and $\beta_3$ should be biased \emph{upward}. We estimate zero.

\subsection{Intraday, and a confound that looks like a result}

We repeat the exercise intraday using $60$ sessions of five-minute bars, the maximum
freely available, aggregated to thirteen half-hour bins ($40{,}560$ bin-observations).
Every programme in the sample launched before this window opens, so there is no
pre/post variation within it and the difference-in-differences design collapses.

A first specification identifies from the interaction of programme size with time of
day across names, with name and date-by-bin effects. It produces a strikingly clean
result: relative to midday, the coefficient rises to $+0.035$ at the open and falls
monotonically to $-0.042$ at 15:00 ($t=-3.52$) and $-0.095$ at 15:30 ($t=-3.20$).

This is not evidence of hedging, and we report it because it is the kind of result that
would be easy to present as such. The sign is wrong: dealer hedging concentrated near
the close predicts \emph{more} close volume in large-programme names, not less. The
pattern is a confound. With programme size time-invariant inside a 60-day window, the
interaction of size with time of day cannot be separated from the interaction of any
other name characteristic with time of day, and programme size is strongly correlated
with clientele. The large-programme names are retail-heavy and open-weighted; the
small-programme names are mega-caps with large closing auctions.

A within-name design removes it. Using each name's own share of daily dollar volume
falling in the final hour, with name and date effects, so that each name's average
intraday shape is absorbed, the interaction of size with weekday is null on all five
days: the largest coefficient is Wednesday at $t=2.19$, against a Bonferroni threshold
near $2.8$ for five tests.

\section{Why is there no footprint?}

The estimates above bound the volume response near zero. They do not explain it. We
offer one explanation we find most plausible and state how it could be tested, and note
two alternatives.

\paragraph{Intermediation against offsetting demand.} The most likely explanation is
that the dealer does not hedge the position into the underlying because it does not
need to. These programmes write calls precisely on the stocks where retail demand to
\emph{buy} calls is heaviest --- the same names dominate both lists. A dealer
intermediating between a fund systematically selling upside and a retail clientele
systematically buying it holds a substantially netted book and hedges only the residual.
Under this account the programme is absorbed by an offsetting clientele rather than by
the underlying's order book, and the absence of a footprint is the expected outcome
rather than a puzzle.

This is a conjecture, and we are careful to label it as such: our data establish the
absence of a footprint, not the reason for it. The natural test uses signed option
volume by trade origin to measure the extent to which programme writing is offset by
customer buying in the same series and expiry. Such data exist commercially. Our free
sources do not identify the counterparty side.

\paragraph{Alternatives.} Two others deserve mention. Delta changes at each roll may be
smaller relative to notional than the at-the-money calculation in Section~2 suggests,
if the overlay is struck far enough out of the money. And hedging may be executed
continuously and patiently enough to leave no dated or time-concentrated trace, in
which case a design keyed on weekday or on time of day is looking in the wrong place
even though the flow is real. We regard the second as the most serious threat to the
interpretation, and it is partially testable as intraday depth accumulates.

\section{Limitations}

We state these plainly because several are material.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Volume, not prices.} We test the necessary condition, not the sufficient
one. A price effect without a volume effect is difficult but not impossible, for
instance through quote revision by liquidity providers who anticipate the flow.

\item \textbf{Roll timing is inferred.} We scan weekdays rather than assume one, which
is an improvement, but we do not observe the day each programme transacts. If rolls are
spread across days or keyed to expiry cycles rather than weekdays, a weekday design
dilutes the estimand. Resolving this requires a holdings time series, which we are
accumulating but do not yet have.

\item \textbf{Intraday depth is 60 sessions.} This is the free limit. The intraday
result should be regarded as preliminary.

\item \textbf{Programme size is a step function.} N-PORT is released quarterly, so size
is measured at a coarser frequency than the outcome.

\item \textbf{Level effects are confounded.} The event study shows a non-flat
pre-trend. We rely on the size response, not on level effects around launch.

\item \textbf{One product family.} Our treated group is dominated by a single issuer's
complex. Index-level and buffer programmes are not covered here.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

A set of option-writing programmes grew to hold, in the largest case, several times
their underlying's entire daily turnover, with positions disclosed daily at contract
level and trades set by a published schedule. Across three orders of magnitude of
programme size, they leave no measurable footprint in the underlying's trading volume,
daily or intraday, while the same estimator detects option-expiry and
volatility-driven effects in the same panel and a randomisation test places the
estimate squarely inside its null.

The natural reading is that a large, uninformed, fully disclosed derivative flow is
intermediated rather than transmitted: absorbed against offsetting demand or by
liquidity provision organised around it, rather than pushed into the underlying's order
book. This aligns with what \citet{brogger2021} finds for a different predictable flow
in a different market, and it suggests that inferences drawn from assumed dealer
hedging should be treated with more caution than is customary.

We also show that the positions concerned are publicly and freely measurable, and that
they reconcile exactly against clearing data. We regard that as the more durable
contribution, and it is available for reuse independent of the conclusion above.
