\title{\textbf{Reading the FLEX Options Market from Clearing Data}\\[0.4em]
\large A Census of Structured Option Positions Invisible to Listed Chains}

\author{Anonymous}

\date{18 August 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
FLEX options are negotiated bilaterally, cleared centrally, and absent from listed
option chains. They are about seven percent of US listed options open interest and grew
forty-five percent in 2025, yet we did not locate empirical work using them. This paper
shows they are observable at no cost and maps them. Listed strikes fall on a standard
grid, so a cleared series struck off that grid was not listed; applying this to 682
underlyings recovers 30.9 million contracts of FLEX open interest, 81\% of the
exchange's own published figure, the shortfall being FLEX struck on-grid which no
strike signature can find. Structures leave separable fingerprints, and two are
verified against fund disclosure: a cent-offset written put is an option-income fund's
synthetic leg, and an off-grid short call is a defined-outcome fund's cap. Counting
cleared series independently, however, mismeasures positions badly, and in both
directions. A butterfly counted leg by leg is credited with 49 times its maximum
attainable value; a four-leg buffer fund is counted nearly three times over; and
strike-based notional records a synthetic long call struck at \$1.87 against a \$765
spot as \$7m when its exposure is \$2.9bn. Grouping legs into positions by their
contract counts and valuing on the underlying gives 1,193 structures across 220
underlyings and \$196bn of exposure, against \$630bn if legs are summed naively.
Composition is roughly even between synthetics, buffer collars and spreads. Because
listed chains omit all of this, gamma measures built from them miss 5.5\% of measured
gamma in aggregate, 0.5\% at the median underlying, and 11.3\% for SPY.

\vspace{0.8em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} FLEX options; clearing data; open interest; defined-outcome
funds; dealer gamma; option market structure.

\noindent\textbf{JEL:} G13, G14, G23.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A FLEX option is negotiated bilaterally between two parties, who then submit it for
central clearing. It combines the customisation of an over-the-counter contract with
the counterparty protection of a cleared one. It does not print on the consolidated
tape and does not appear in the option chains that both practitioners and researchers
use, and it is consequently treated as unobservable.

The market is not small. Cboe reports FLEX open interest of 38.1 million contracts for
2025, up 45\% year on year and about 7\% of total US listed options open
interest.\footnote{Cboe Global Markets, ``Index FLEX Contracts Hit New Records as
Investors Customize Their Options''.} Despite this we did not locate empirical work
using FLEX data, in searches across the usual repositories. The absence is plausibly
explained by the assumption that the data does not exist.

It does. The Options Clearing Corporation publishes open interest for every series it
clears, FLEX included, and FLEX is separable from listed activity by a simple property:
listed strikes fall on a standard grid, so a cleared series whose strike sits off that
grid was not listed. This paper applies that observation to 682 underlyings and reports
what is there.

\paragraph{What we find.} Three things.

First, the method works and can be checked. It recovers 30.9 million contracts carrying
the FLEX strike signature, which is 81\% of the exchange's own published figure. The
shortfall is expected and in the conservative direction: FLEX struck on the standard
grid cannot be identified by strike alone, so the census is a lower bound.

Second, structures are separable and the separation can be verified. Defined-outcome
funds, option-income funds, financing structures and negotiated blocks each leave a
distinct fingerprint, and two of the fingerprints are confirmed against fund
disclosure without having been derived from it.

Third, and most consequential for anyone using open-interest data of any kind: counting
cleared series independently mismeasures positions severely. We document three
instances with magnitudes, correct them by grouping legs into positions, and report the
corrected census.

\paragraph{Why the third point generalises.} Open interest is reported per series. A
position is not a series. When the two are conflated, a structure is counted once per
leg and valued at a strike that may bear no relation to its exposure. We found this
three times in succession while building the census --- in a fund's synthetic long, in
a butterfly, and in a buffer collar --- with errors of 49-fold, threefold, and three
orders of magnitude respectively. The errors have opposite signs and do not cancel.
Since the same conflation is available to any study using open interest, we report the
failures in detail rather than only the corrected figures.

\paragraph{Relation to the literature.} A long line of work infers option-market
positioning from listed data under assumptions about who holds each side.
\citet{bollen2004} read net buying pressure from the shape of the implied volatility
function; \citet{garleanu2009} model demand pressure directly and estimate it from
exchange data identifying end users and intermediaries; \citet{ni2005} use aggregate
open interest at strikes to identify clustering of stock prices on expiration dates.
Each takes the set of observable series as given. Our contribution is orthogonal to the
sign problem those papers confront, and does not solve it: we add a category of
positions previously assumed unobservable, and show that the standard practice of
treating each series as a position mismeasures what is already observed. The
expiration-date literature is the most directly affected, since strike-level open
interest is its input and we show that input to be incomplete in a way that is
concentrated in particular underlyings.

The single-name option-income funds that first motivated this study are examined by
\citet{park2025}, who find that aggregate call overwriting by income funds predicts
lower implied and realised volatility in the following month. Our census locates those
funds within the wider FLEX market and finds them to be a small share of it, which is
context for their result rather than a challenge to it.

\paragraph{What this paper does not do.} It is a census taken on one date. It says
nothing about growth, turnover or dynamics, which require a time series we are
accumulating but do not have. It makes no claim about returns or profitability. And,
for a reason inherent to the data rather than to our method, it cannot say who holds
what: clearing data reports open interest per series aggregated across all holders, so
a position identified here is the sum of every participant in that series.

\section{Institutional background and data}

\subsection{Why FLEX exists and why it is invisible}

FLEX contracts let a user set strike, expiry, exercise style and settlement outside
the listed grid. The natural users are those whose requirements do not fit standard
series: a defined-outcome fund that must strike a cap at a level derived from an index
value on a particular morning; an income fund that needs a series which will not be
netted against a listed one at clearing; a desk transporting cash through a box spread
at a strike far from spot.

Because the trade is negotiated rather than executed on a lit book, it does not
generate a print. Because the resulting series is not listed, it does not appear in an
option chain. What it does generate is a cleared position, and clearing is where it
becomes visible.

\subsection{Sources}

Everything used here is free and public.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Clearing.} OCC series files give open interest per series per underlying,
      including FLEX and including non-standard deliverable roots.
\item \textbf{Listed chains.} An exchange content delivery network publishes full
      chains with two-sided quotes, sizes, implied volatility and greeks. Comparing
      these against clearing data isolates what the chain cannot see.
\item \textbf{Disclosure.} SEC Form N-PORT gives registered funds' option positions at
      contract level, and EDGAR full-text search indexes it. This is how signatures
      are verified.
\item \textbf{Prices.} A public charting interface supplies spot and daily bars.
\end{itemize}

The universe is the 682 underlyings with listed weekly options, comprising 486,895
cleared series and 576 million contracts of open interest on 17 August 2026.

\subsection{Identifying FLEX}

Listed strikes fall on a standard grid: whole dollars, halves, quarters. Define a
series as off-grid when the cents component of its strike is not $0$, $25$, $50$ or
$75$. Applying this to the universe isolates 5,084 series carrying 30.9 million
contracts across 326 of the 682 underlyings.

Two checks support the identification.

\paragraph{Against the exchange.} Cboe reports 38.1 million FLEX contracts for 2025.
The detector finds 30.9 million on a single day in 2026, or 81\% of that figure. Since
FLEX may also be struck on the grid, where no strike signature can find it, the census
is a lower bound and the direction of the gap is as expected.

\paragraph{Against the listed chain.} Matching clearing series to chain series on
expiry and strike, 90.8\% of clearing open interest corresponds to a listed series and
9.2\% does not. Of the total, 5.5\% carries the off-grid signature and 5.7\% sits at
expiries the chain does not carry at all; the categories overlap. No open interest is
left unexplained on the standard grid at expiries the chain does carry, so the
decomposition closes.

An intermediate version of this comparison put the invisible share at 16.7\% by
comparing aggregate sums rather than matching series, which counted open-interest
differences on matched series as missing series. The series-level figure of 9.2\%
supersedes it.

\section{A taxonomy of structures}

Categories are assigned from structural signatures alone. Two are then verified against
disclosure that played no part in constructing them.

\begin{description}
\item[Fund synthetic] A one- or two-cent offset from a round strike with open interest
concentrated on the put side. Single-name option-income funds hold a synthetic long ---
long call, short put, effectively one strike --- and write calls against it; the cent
offset keeps the written put from being netted against the listed series at clearing.

\item[Index-linked] An arbitrary decimal strike, such as SPY at 765.66 or 597.42.
Defined-outcome funds set caps and floors from an index level on the day an outcome
period opens, which produces strikes that cannot be on a grid. \emph{20.6\% of these
series expire on a month end, against 4.7\% for every other category} --- the monthly
outcome-period cycle appearing in the data without being assumed.

\item[Matched combination] Call and put open interest exactly equal in one series: a
conversion, reversal or box. These are financing and carry structures. Their legs
offset, so they carry no directional or convex exposure.

\item[Block] A one-sided round lot on a standard strike, large enough to be a single
negotiated trade rather than an accumulation.
\end{description}

\subsection{Verification against disclosure}

Attributed fund-synthetic legs reconcile to issuer holdings exactly, to the contract,
in 8 of 11 cases. Of the three that do not, one differs by 116 contracts, consistent
with a second holder in the series; one by 4,009, consistent with a second programme at
the same strike; and one is a false positive on the call side.

Two structures were traced to named filings by searching N-PORT and reading the
document rather than trusting the search result:

Both confirm the signature assigned without reference to disclosure. The Innovator
filing also carries the identifier \texttt{4SPY 261130C00765660}, showing that
defined-outcome issuers use leading-digit non-standard roots, the same convention as
the income funds.

\subsection{The limits of attribution}

Attribution does not scale, for three reasons that are worth stating because they bound
what this data can ever deliver.

Search hits are not attributions. Pursuing a structure at strike 230.01 returned a
filing holding \texttt{JPM} at that strike rather than the underlying sought: strike
values recur across underlyings and full-text search cannot reliably conjoin
underlying, strike and expiry. Round strikes return the search limit of ten thousand
hits and are useless --- which is precisely where the unattributed block exposure sits.

Most fundamentally, clearing data is aggregated across holders. The SPY 765.66 series
carries 37,507 contracts and the Innovator fund holds 4,473 of them. A confirmed match
therefore identifies \emph{a} holder, never \emph{the} holder. Every position reported
below is a sum across participants.

\section{Counting series is not counting positions}

Building the census surfaced the same error three times. Because each produced
plausible output, and because the same mistake is available to any study using open
interest, we report them.

\subsection{A butterfly counted leg by leg}

One underlying in the census carries a ladder of one-sided call lines:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Expiry & \multicolumn{3}{c}{Strike (contracts)} \\
\midrule
2026-11-20 & 510.01 (100k) & 550.01 (200k) & 590.01 (100k) \\
2026-12-17 & 525.00 (100k) & 575.00 (200k) & 625.00 (100k) \\
2027-01-14 & 430.00 (100k) & 470.00 (200k) & 510.00 (100k) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Three strikes at equal spacing with open interest in a 1:2:1 ratio is a butterfly. Read
as nine independent lines it is \$63.8bn of notional. A butterfly cannot be worth more
than its strike spacing times its wing size, which is \$1.30bn in total: an
overstatement of 49 times. These are the only butterflies in 682 underlyings, so this is
one identifiable programme rather than a pattern, but the defect it revealed was
general.

\subsection{A buffer fund counted four times}

SPY at expiry 2026-09-30 carries 99 off-grid legs. One fund is visible across four of
them because the contract counts match:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rlrl}
\toprule
Strike & Side & Contracts & Role \\
\midrule
1.87 & call & 37,507 & synthetic long \\
765.66 & call & 37,507 & cap \\
746.77 & put & 38,317 & floor \\
597.42 & put & 37,540 & buffer floor \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The position is roughly 37,500 contracts of SPY, about \$2.87bn. Counted leg by leg at
strike-based notional it is \$8.02bn.

\subsection{Notional fails in both directions at once}

The same fund demonstrates both failure modes. Its four legs inflate the count. But its
synthetic long call, struck at \$1.87 against a spot near \$765, carries \$2.9bn of
exposure and is recorded by strike times contracts as \$7m --- three orders of magnitude
too small. Deep in-the-money calls used to replicate an underlying are systematically
invisible to a strike-based measure, while far out-of-the-money legs are inflated by it.

A third case makes the point without a fund: the largest single line in the census is an
SPY series struck at \textbf{10,010.01} against a spot near 765, with equal call and put
open interest and \$29bn of notional. It is a box spread. Its economic content is a
loan.

\section{The corrected census}

\subsection{Method}

Legs of one trade carry near-identical contract counts at a shared expiry, because they
were transacted together. Clustering on contract count within an expiry recovers the
position without requiring the number of structures to be known in advance. Each
recovered structure is then valued once, on the underlying, as contracts $\times$ spot
--- the only scale that survives both failure modes above.

Table~\ref{tab:census} reports 1,193 structures across 220 underlyings and \$196bn of
exposure, against \$630bn if legs are summed naively: an overstatement of 3.2 times.
The method reproduces the case worked by hand, valuing the SPY buffer fund at \$2.90bn
against \$2.87bn manual and \$8.02bn per leg.

Composition is roughly even across three families. Synthetics and buffer collars each
account for a quarter to a third, spreads a fifth. The single-name option-income funds
that motivated this study are a small part of the market they belong to.

Largest individual positions are three vertical spreads on one underlying of \$4.8bn
each across consecutive expiries, a spread of \$4.69bn expiring in 2028, and the SPY
buffer at \$2.90bn.

\subsection{What the grouping does not capture}

Only 3,291 of 5,084 legs group, so \$196bn is itself a partial count; the remainder are
singletons or fall below the size threshold and are left unvalued. Clustering on size
also separates a butterfly's body from its wings, because the body is twice their size,
so the butterflies appear here as vertical spreads while the dedicated leg-level
detector finds them correctly. We report both rather than reconciling them by hand.
And grouping is inference: legs are assigned to one structure because their sizes
match, which is strong evidence but not proof, and only the fund-synthetic cases are
independently verified.

\section{Implication for gamma measurement}

Measures of dealer gamma in public use are built from listed chains and therefore
exclude every position above. Pricing the absent series under Black-Scholes at implied
volatility interpolated from the nearest listed strikes in the same expiry, and
cancelling matched legs because a box contributes no gamma:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
& Share of listed-chain gamma \\
\midrule
Aggregate, 67 underlyings & 5.5\% \\
Median underlying & 0.5\% \\
SPY & 11.3\% \\
Largest cases & 25--43\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The omission is concentrated rather than pervasive. For a typical underlying it is
negligible. For SPY --- the contract most gamma commentary concerns --- roughly one
dollar of gamma in nine is invisible to the data such commentary is built on.

An earlier version of this calculation reported a median of 3.6\%. It priced every
off-grid strike at the chain's median implied volatility, which badly overstates gamma
for far out-of-the-money strikes, and counted both legs of matched combinations that in
fact cancel; cancelling matched legs alone removes 29\% of the gross figure. The
corrected figures supersede it.

We stress that this is a statement about measurement inputs, not about true dealer
positioning. Attributing a sign to open interest requires knowing who holds each side,
which neither listed nor clearing data reveals. That problem is unchanged by this paper;
what changes is that a category of inputs previously assumed unavailable is available.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{One date.} Everything is a snapshot. Growth, turnover and entry dynamics
require a time series.
\item \textbf{A lower bound.} FLEX struck on the standard grid is undetectable by strike
signature, and only 65\% of detected legs group into structures.
\item \textbf{Aggregated across holders.} Clearing data cannot identify participants;
every position is a sum.
\item \textbf{Categories are inferred.} Two signatures are verified against disclosure;
the others rest on structural evidence that is strong but not proof.
\item \textbf{No claim about prices.} This paper measures positions. Whether they
matter for returns or volatility is not tested here.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

FLEX options are about seven percent of US listed options open interest, growing
quickly, and generally treated as unobservable. They are observable at no cost, because
clearing reports what execution does not, and because a strike off the standard grid
identifies itself.

Mapping them yields \$196bn of structured positions across 220 underlyings, split
roughly evenly between synthetics, defined-outcome collars and spreads, none of which
appears in a listed option chain. Reaching that figure required correcting a
threefold overstatement caused by counting legs rather than positions, and the same
correction reveals a butterfly overstated 49-fold and a synthetic long call understated
by three orders of magnitude.

That methodological point may outlast the census. Open interest is reported per series;
positions are not series; and the errors from conflating them are large, of both signs,
and do not cancel.
